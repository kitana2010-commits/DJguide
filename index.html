the you in the story —

Your Persona

Before you chat with anyone, you decide who you are. Not the bot — you. The character you're playing as. A persona tells the AI who it's actually talking to, and the difference between "having one" and "not having one" is huge.

What it is — the short version

A user persona is the character you wear when you chat with a bot. Name, basic appearance, vibe, voice — whatever the bot needs to know about you to actually talk to you instead of guessing.

Without a persona, the AI invents details about you on the fly. That's why some bots default to addressing you as a generic stranger, or randomly assume things about you that don't match who you wanted to be in the story. A persona is how you stop being a stranger.

two personas, don't confuse them

The bot's Persona (over in Character Bots → Persona) is the brain of them. Your Persona (this page) is the brain of you. Same word, totally different jobs. We didn't name them.

Why bother

  • The bot can actually talk to you. Use your name, react to your traits, mirror your vibe.
  • You can play someone different. Soft and shy in one chat, ruthless and witty in another — whatever the story wants.
  • Less random nonsense. The AI stops inventing details about you that you never agreed to.
  • Immersion goes up. The story feels like it's happening to someone instead of a faceless camera floating in the room.

Where to set it up

Look for the Personas area in your Dreamjourney account — usually near your profile or settings. From there you can create one, edit it, or switch between several.

tip

[ Drop the exact in-app path here if you'd like — e.g. "Profile icon → Personas → New Persona." Different platforms move this around, so it's worth checking. ]

What to actually put in it

Keep it tight. A user persona doesn't need to be a novel — it just needs to give the AI enough to talk to.

good to include
  • Name (or alias, or just {{user}})
  • Pronouns
  • Basic appearance — a few brushstrokes, not a portrait
  • 2–3 personality traits
  • Speech style, if it matters
  • Anything the bot must know up front (job, age range, relationship status)
usually skip
  • Real-life personal info (full name, address, anything you wouldn't tell a stranger)
  • Mood for today (that's Context)
  • A full life story (too much, it'll fixate)
  • Exact current outfit (same trap as bot Personas — they'll wear it forever)
the golden rule, again

The same wisdom from the bot's Persona applies here: less is more. A user persona that's 4–5 sentences tends to work better than one that's a full character sheet. The AI references it constantly, and a long persona crowds the context window for no real gain.

You can have more than one

You're not locked into a single persona forever. Most people end up with a few:

  • A "default you" — close to who you actually are, for casual chats
  • A fantasy you — for romance, adventure, the dramatic stuff
  • A blank slate with just {{user}} and pronouns, for when you want the bot to project less on you

Swap between them depending on the bot and the mood. Some platforms let you assign a default persona per-bot, which is great once you have a couple you like.

example

Name: Wren. Pronouns: she/they. Late twenties, sharp-eyed, dresses softly but isn't soft. Quick-witted, gets sarcastic when nervous, asks questions before making moves. Tends to read people more than they read her.

Notice what's not there: no current outfit, no current mood, no backstory paragraph, no list of skills. Just enough for the bot to know who walked into the room.

A note on privacy — please read this one

Your persona lives in chats with the AI, but anything you type there is processed by the model and may be stored. Don't put real personal info in your persona — full legal name, address, workplace, anything you wouldn't share with a stranger. A first name or alias is fine. Pronouns are fine. Vibes are fine. Identifying details are not.

Character Bots

Building a bot is just describing a person well. Voice, vibe, the little things they'd never say out loud. Each tab below is one piece of the puzzle — start with Persona if you're new.

This is the brain of your character. The Persona section is where you define what makes them them. The bot will pull from this constantly, and things written here get referenced over and over again — so only include stuff that's permanent or core to who they are.

good to include
  • Personality traits
  • Important beliefs
  • Moral alignment
  • Speech patterns
usually skip
  • Current emotions
  • Temporary outfits / clothes
  • Exact scene details
  • Other characters (unless extremely important)
  • Direct references to {{user}}

Why does this matter? Because the AI treats the Persona like a constant reference point. Drop temporary info here and the bot will keep dragging it back into scenes over and over.

cautionary tale

If you mention a leather jacket here, the bot may put them in that jacket constantly — even if you set it on fire and throw it off a cliff. It'll be back.

If you mention another character here, the bot may keep dragging them into scenes. If you say they're angry here, they may stay angry forever. This section does a lot of the heavy lifting, so keep it clean and focused.

the golden rule

Keep this section to roughly 6–7 sentences max. Too much personality info actually makes the bot less consistent — the more overloaded it gets, the harder it is for the AI to figure out what matters most.

The three big things

  • Use plain language
  • Write naturally
  • Be clear, and leave little room for misinterpretation

Don't be afraid to ask Schmoo or ChatGPT to read your Persona and tell you how they think the character would behave. It's a great gut-check — if the AI describes them differently than you intended, your core may be unclear.

Adding just 2–3 open-ended questions to your Persona, Instructions, or Details can seriously improve your bot's consistency. Why? They nudge the LLM to actually think about the character instead of mechanically copying traits — which reduces moments where the bot suddenly feels out of character or starts sounding like a generic AI chatbot.

Examples that work

  • How does Bob react when he realizes he's losing control of a situation?
  • What kinds of people tend to frustrate Bob the fastest, and why?
  • In what small ways does Bob unintentionally reveal that he's lonely?
  • How would Bob justify doing something morally wrong if he believed it was necessary?
  • How does the world treat people born with magic, and how has that shaped Bob's view of others?
less is more

You do not need a lot of these. Usually 2–3 strong questions works way better than adding twenty random ones. Too many can confuse the model and make the character feel unfocused. Keep them meaningful and relevant — they carry more weight when your Persona is short (remember the 6–7 sentence rule).

Not as critical as the Persona, but still matters a lot. Think of Details as the place for supporting info that fleshes the character out.

Stuff that fits here

  • Appearance
  • Age
  • Job
  • Other characters (family, friends, etc.)
  • Backstory and important memories
  • Speech style and nuances
  • Habits

Avoid exact outfits

If you say the character is wearing a black leather jacket, the AI may start acting like they wear that jacket constantly. Describe their general style instead.

better

He dresses sharply and prefers dark clothing.

worse

He's wearing a black leather jacket, ripped jeans, and combat boots.

You can have a little more fun in this section — fill it up if you want, but keep in mind: less is more. If you max it out, the LLM will almost definitely skip over and miss things occasionally.

Don't repeat information you already wrote in the Persona unless it's extremely important. Repetition muddies the bot and wastes context space.

One of the most important parts of the bot. The first message teaches the AI what the roleplay should feel like. Mood, pacing, writing style, character behavior, overall vibe — all heavily influenced by this section.

This is also the one place where longer writing is usually a good thing. Add detail: where the character is, what just happened before the scene started, what mood they're in, how they speak, what they're doing, what the environment feels like. The clearer the scene, the easier it is for the AI to stay consistent.

remember

The AI usually copies the style of the first message. Long and descriptive opening? Replies will trend longer and more descriptive. Short and fast-paced? It'll keep writing that way. If you use italics for actions, expect italics throughout. Dramatic short paragraphs? Same.

Don't write for the user — biggest trap

If you tell the user exactly what they say, feel, or do, the AI becomes more likely to keep controlling them later in the roleplay.

controlling

{{user}} walked into the room confidently and flirted with him.

leaves room

The door opened, drawing his attention toward the person standing in the doorway.

The second version lets the user decide who they are and how they act — and more importantly, it keeps the LLM from writing for {{user}} in future replies.

A quick word on POV

  • AnyPOV — gender-neutral writing; anyone can use the bot
  • FemPOV — user is written as female
  • MalePOV — user is written as male

The Context field is for temporary information. Think of it like short-term instructions for the AI that fall off after only 5–10 messages. It's the right place to put things you want the bot to remember or focus on at the start of a roleplay.

Use it for

  • Temporary behavior
  • Scene setup
  • Short-term mood changes
  • Current situations
  • Activating lorebook entries immediately
example {{char}} is trying to hide an injury.
or {{char}} is in an aggressive mood after having a terrible day.
not for permanent stuff

Unlike the Persona, this field is NOT meant for permanent character traits. Think of it as "what is happening right now?" rather than "who is this character at their core?"

This is the bot's public info page — where users learn about the character, setting, and roleplay before they start chatting. Use it to show off your character and get creative. Pretty, informative, dramatic, aesthetic, immersive — whatever fits your bot best.

Explain the world, the important relationships, the character's vibe, their story, or anything else you want users to know before stepping in. The section supports Markdown formatting, so you can really customize it: images, bold, italics, headers, bullets, emojis, links, even clickable images. A lot of creators use this space to promote collaborations, link bots into collections or universes, share playlists, or just make their profile stand out.

important caveat

This box carries absolutely no weight when it comes to how the AI will behave. It's purely for human readers — don't put behavioral instructions here expecting them to stick.

Scenes are basically alternate first messages. They let you create different roleplay scenarios without needing to make entirely separate bots.

Since the first message heavily affects tone, behavior, pacing, and writing style, scenes are a great way to explore different sides of a character — or completely different story setups. Each scene has its own first message and can have a totally different vibe from the default. One scene could be romantic, another enemies-to-lovers, another horror, another an AU — all while keeping your character intact.

Scene Context vs Scene Instructions

scene context

Works just like the normal Context field — mostly for temporary instructions, current situations, moods, or short-term behaviors. Usually fades after 5–10 messages.

scene instructions

Much stronger. Can heavily affect tone, behavior, writing style, and even personality during that specific scene. Not as powerful as Author's Notes, but still carries a lot of weight — so use it carefully instead of stuffing it full of information.

building a world —

Scenario Bots

When the world matters more than any one character. A different beast from character bots, but not as scary as it sounds.

Scenario bots aren't that different from character bots, but they can feel more intimidating at first because they're written a little differently.

character bot

Built around one specific person. The roleplay focuses on that character, their personality, their relationship with the user, and how they react to what's happening.

scenario bot

Built around the situation, setting, or world. The user has more room to explore, interact with different people, and move through the story without one character constantly pulling the scene back to themselves.

You can absolutely turn a character bot into a scenario bot. The main difference is focus. Character bot: the character is the center. Scenario bot: the world or situation is the center.

to illustrate

A character bot might be about talking to a vampire prince. A scenario bot might be about surviving in a kingdom secretly ruled by vampires — the prince can still exist, but the entire story doesn't have to revolve around him every single message.

The easiest way to write instructions for a scenario bot is to think like a director. You're not just describing one character anymore — you're telling the AI what kind of world this is and how it should behave.

Questions to ask yourself

  • Is the world peaceful, dangerous, magical, modern, broken, strict, romantic, haunted, or politically tense?
  • Are people kind to strangers, or do they distrust outsiders?
  • Is the world bright and hopeful, or dark and survival-based?

This section works a lot like the Persona box for a character bot. It carries a lot of weight, and the AI will reference it often — so only put things here that you actually want showing up frequently.

consequence

If you say it's storming, the AI may keep bringing up rain, thunder, wet streets, and dark skies over and over. If you mention the kingdom is at war, expect war to affect the way people talk, travel, trade, and react to strangers.

Focus on the core idea of the world, not every tiny detail. The AI needs to understand the "main rule" of the setting first. Details can always be added later in lorebooks, scenes, or context boxes.

Just like with character bots, the Details section is where you put extra information that supports the main instructions. A good place for things the AI should know, but that don't need to be the main focus every single message.

Good things to include

  • Important characters
  • Towns, kingdoms, factions
  • Political groups, points of interest
  • Religions, guilds, species
  • Magic systems, technology
  • Social rules

Keep it organized and easy to understand. Don't dump every tiny fact here. If the info is too long or only matters sometimes, it'll work better as a lorebook entry.

Try not to repeat what you wrote in the main Scenario Instructions. Instructions explain the core of the world. Details should add useful supporting info around it.

example

If your instructions say the kingdom is divided by war, the Details section could explain the names of the two sides, what they believe, and which towns or characters belong to each.

Keep it simple. The AI does better when important information is clear instead of buried under a mountain of lore.

How your bot sounds

Prose is just a fancy word for normal writing — how a character talks, narrates, or describes things in sentences and paragraphs. The good news? You can absolutely tell a bot how to write. Just describe the prose style you want in its guts and watch the whole vibe shift.

The seven flavors — pick your poison

Purple
Very detailed and dramatic. Lots of description and emotion.
"The storm clawed against the windows as his voice dropped to a dangerous whisper."
Minimalist
Clean and simple. Fewer details. Straight to the point.
"He shut the door and sat down. 'We need to talk.'"
Poetic
Artistic and lyrical. Focuses on rhythm, imagery, and emotion.
"The rain sang against the glass like a mourning hymn."
Gritty
Harsh, raw, realistic. Often used for crime, horror, or dark fantasy.
"Smoke burned his lungs. Blood dried on his knuckles."
Cinematic
Feels like a movie scene. Strong visuals and dramatic pacing.
"Neon lights reflected off the wet pavement as the city buzzed below."
Flowery
Extremely decorative — heavy on adjectives and embellishment.
"Golden sunlight cascaded through the delicate lace curtains."
Staccato
Very short, punchy sentences. Fast pacing. Strong impact.
"He looked up. Saw her. Froze."
Oh my god — I hate Staccato. How do I make it stop?help —

If it's your bot, you can simply add a little prompt into your persona or author's note:

try this Prose: Write in standard paragraphs using complete sentences.

If it's someone else's bot, you'll have to rely on OOC notes and your system prompt. But fair warning: if the creator has already prompted hard for a specific prose style, you might be fighting it the entire chat.

long-term memory —

Lorebooks

Worlds that remember. Lorebooks are where your story keeps its own secrets — but they can also flood your chat if you're not careful. Here's the whole picture.

A lorebook is where you store extra information the bot doesn't need all the time. Think of it like a storage box for worldbuilding, side characters, locations, factions, history, or important rules. Instead of stuffing everything into the bot's main prompt, you can put longer or more specific info in the lorebook.

Lorebooks work with trigger words. When the user or bot says a trigger word, the matching entry gets pulled into the roleplay.

example

If you have a lorebook entry for "Velaris," you might use trigger words like: Velaris or City of Starlight. Or, if your character has an entry for their mother Carol, your trigger words might be: Carol, mother, and mom.

My lorebook won't let me save!troubleshoot —

Check these things first:

  • Do all of your entries have trigger words, a category type, an entry name, and at least something in the description?
  • Are you maxing out all your entries? Try shortening them — keep each one under about 800 characters.
  • Did you import a JSON file? Sometimes it bugs out. Try again, or add each entry by hand and save frequently.

The most important rule with trigger words: don't use the same trigger word for a bunch of different entries. If five lorebook entries all share one trigger word, the bot may try to pull all five at once. That floods the context and eats up way too much space.

When context gets too crowded, the LLM has a harder time knowing what matters. It may start ignoring parts of the lorebook, mixing details together, or acting like it didn't pull the entry at all.

Make them specific

Instead of "Kingdom" for every kingdom-related entry, use names like Camelot or Kingdom of Camelot. For family members — if your character has multiple siblings — add a small section in the Details:

in the details Brothers: John, Carl, Bob, James Sisters: Caitlin, Heather, Rebecca, Samantha

Then use just their names as trigger words. Inside the description of each lorebook entry, you can reiterate: "{{char}}'s older brother," etc.

But wait — if you put trigger words inside your bot's guts (like that sibling list from the previous tab), isn't that also pulling entries unintentionally? It absolutely can, yes! That's called cascade or context flooding, and there's a clever way around it.

The underscore trick

Adding an underscore before or after a trigger word can stop the lorebook entry from activating by accident. If you have John as a trigger word, in your details you can write:

try this _John or John_

This is useful when you want the bot to have access to extra information, but you don't want it to potentially trigger the lorebook entry just from being mentioned in the guts of the bot.

It works really well for character lists, locations, factions, hidden lore, or background information that should only appear when you specifically want it.

tiny con

Very rarely, the LLM might refer to "John" as "_John" in a reply. A simple reroll fixes it. In my opinion the benefits outweigh the rare, minor inconvenience — but now you know it can happen.

Dreamscapes

The audio-visual layer that pairs with your bot. When someone turns one on, the scene generates around what's happening — a face, a setting, an atmosphere — to deepen the immersion.

A Dreamscape is an audio-visual environment you attach to your bot. Other users can flip it on when they chat with your bot, and the scene generates around what's happening in the roleplay — character closeups, location shots, atmospheric vibes — all to deepen the immersion.

Think of it like the soundtrack and cinematography of your bot. The writing is still doing the heavy lifting, but a good Dreamscape turns "a chat" into "a scene I'm inside of."

heads up

At the moment, in-chat Dreamscapes are temporarily removed from the platform per Dreamjourney's own docs. The editor still works for setting them up, so your bot will be ready when they're switched back on for chats.

The Dreamscape editor lives on your bot's page, behind the Creator Options menu. Here's the path:

  • Open your bot's page
  • Click Creator Options
  • Click Edit Dreamscape

That lands you on the Edit Dreamscape page, where you can build a fresh one or tweak an existing one.

What the generator pulls from — it's not just your instructions

Behind the curtain, the Dreamscape uses a few different sources at once:

  • The visual instructions you write in the Dreamscape itself
  • The bot's description — all those appearance and world details from Persona and Details
  • Any reference images you upload
  • Other context from the chat at the moment of generation
expect a little wobble

Generative AI is wobbly by nature — even with perfect instructions, the same scene can come out slightly different each run. That's normal. Aim for the core elements being right, not pixel-perfect consistency.

There are two main flavors of Dreamscape, and they want slightly different things from you.

Character-focused — the face matters

When the Dreamscape is meant to show off your character, the bot description does most of the work. Make sure these are spelled out clearly somewhere in the bot:

  • Eye color
  • Hair color and style
  • Notable physical features (scars, tattoos, build, posture)

A clear, high-quality reference image helps a lot — give the AI a face to lock onto.

try this instruction focus, closeup on {charactername}

That nudges the generator to keep the camera on the character instead of drifting into wide environment shots.

Scenery-focused — the world matters

When the bot is more about the world than any one face — a tavern, a battlefield, a haunted forest — describe the atmosphere, lighting, and key environmental elements in the bot. Then tell the Dreamscape to leave the camera off characters:

try this instruction focus on scenery, no characters

Mood, time of day, weather, materials, scale — all worth mentioning. The more specific the world, the better the scene.

Why does my Dreamscape keep coming out wrong?troubleshoot —

Most "wrong" outputs come from one of three places:

  • The bot description is too vague about appearance or setting — the AI is filling gaps with guesses
  • The reference image is low quality, multi-character, or contradicts the description
  • The visual instructions are pulling in two directions (e.g. closeup + wide shot at the same time)

Tighten one of those and try again. It usually takes a couple of passes.

where the art comes from —

Genning Your Images

The art on your bot's profile, the headers in your AC, the secondary characters in your scenes — they all have to come from somewhere. Here's where, how, and the single biggest rule to keep in mind.

Is it too realistic for DJAI? — the big question

Short answer: if it could pass for a real photograph of a real person, it's too real. Like nearly every roleplay platform, DJAI is built around illustrated characters — anime, painted portraits, digital art, semi-realistic but clearly stylized. Photo-realistic AI art crosses a line because it stops being obviously fictional, and that opens doors nobody wants opened.

The good news: you have an enormous amount of room before you hit "too real." Anime, painterly, cel-shaded, watercolor, comic-style, semi-realism — all completely fair game and honestly more fun anyway. Style is personality, and your bot benefits from looking like someone made it on purpose.

the rule of thumb

Look at your image and ask: "could a stranger believe this is a candid photo of a real person?" If yes, restyle it. If it obviously reads as illustration or art, you're fine. When in doubt, push it more stylized, not less.

platform specifics

[ Drop the exact DJAI policy wording here when you have it — file size limits, banned categories, etc. We're working from the general roleplay-platform principle. ]

What styles work great

When you're prompting an image generator (or commissioning art, or pulling from a stock pool), these style anchors keep you safely in "obviously art" territory:

  • anime style / manga style — classic for character bots
  • digital painting / digital illustration — painterly, expressive
  • concept art — cinematic, often great for scenario bots
  • cel-shaded / flat color — clean, modern, very stylized
  • watercolor / ink wash — soft, atmospheric, distinctive
  • semi-realism — realistic proportions and lighting, but still painterly
  • storybook illustration — warm, narrative, fairy-tale energy

What to keep out of your prompt

These are the phrases that drag generators toward photo-realism. If you see them in a prompt template, strip them out:

  • photograph, photorealistic, hyperrealistic
  • RAW photo, DSLR, candid, shot on iPhone
  • 8k, ultra HD (these tend to pull toward photo-finish quality)
  • real skin, real person, professional headshot
  • Specific real camera names (Canon, Sony, etc.)

And in the negative prompt (if your generator supports one), it's worth adding photo, photograph, realistic, real person as an extra safeguard.

Where to make your images

There are a lot of options. These are the ones the community uses most — different strengths for different jobs.

NovelAI

Anime/manga-focused, paid subscription. Probably the most popular tool for character-bot art in this community.

Stable Diffusion

Free if run locally, lots of free hosted versions. Massive variety of models — pick one trained on the style you want.

Midjourney

Discord-based, subscription, beautiful out of the box. Easy to use, harder to keep consistent across multiple images.

Pixai / Tensor

Browser-based, free tiers, strong anime models. Friendly for newcomers who don't want to install anything.

Civitai

A library of community-made Stable Diffusion models. Find a style you love, run it via whichever SD frontend you prefer.

Human artists

Commissions still exist! For your "main" bot art, hiring a human artist on Twitter, Bsky, or Ko-fi gets you something nobody else has.

Keeping your bot's images consistent

The trick to a polished-looking bot is using the same style across all its images — main portrait, scene art, AC long image, any secondary characters. The image generator has no memory between runs, so consistency comes from you reusing a "style anchor" prompt.

a style anchor you reuse soft digital painting, muted color palette, painterly brushwork, gentle lighting, warm tones, anime-influenced

Paste that same style anchor into every prompt for that bot. The subject changes (closeup, full body, scene, secondary character) but the style stays locked. That's what makes a bot's image set look made instead of found.

Specs & sizing

A few practical defaults that work well across DJAI's image slots:

  • Profile / bot avatar — square crop, around 1024×1024px
  • AC header / banner — roughly 16:9, around 1600×900px
  • Long AC images — tall, around 1:3 to 1:5 ratio, ~1000px wide
  • Scene art — landscape 16:9 or 4:3, around 1500×1000px
  • Save as JPG for photos and paintings (small files), PNG for crisp edges and transparency

The two hard "no's"

no real people

We're giving up all our little secrets — the prompt moves, the behavior hacks, how to put together those pretty long images you sometimes see in author's comments, and more. Four tabs. Wander wherever's most useful to you.

How you write the bot matters more than how much you write. These are the moves that get more out of the same number of tokens.

Open-ended questions — the cheat code

Sprinkling 2–3 open-ended questions into the Persona or Details is one of the highest-leverage moves in the whole guide. It nudges the LLM to think about your character instead of mechanically copying traits — and that's what makes a bot stop sounding like a chatbot.

try this in a Persona

How does she behave when she realizes she's losing? What kinds of people frustrate her the fastest, and why?

Two questions like that often outperform a dozen extra trait words. More on open-ended questions →

Specific names beat generic descriptors

The LLM treats specific proper nouns as anchors. "The kingdom" gets blurry; "the Kingdom of Velaris" becomes a thing the model holds onto. Same with characters — "her brother" drifts, "her brother Cal" sticks.

sticks

Cal, her older brother, runs the smithy in the Lower Ward.

drifts

Her older brother runs a smithy in the city.

Show, don't just describe

If you want the bot to talk a specific way, give it an example. One short line of dialogue in the bot's voice is worth a paragraph of "she speaks dryly with a flat affect." LLMs imitate examples extremely well.

in a Persona or Details Example of how she speaks: "If you'd wanted help, you'd have asked nicely. You didn't. So."

Use comparisons to disambiguate

Vague traits like "stoic" or "cold" can land in fifty different ways. Compare to something specific to lock the meaning in:

try this

Stoic like a tired ER doctor, not stoic like a robot. He's not unfeeling — he's just out of bandwidth.

Use an AI as your beta reader

Once your Persona is written, paste it into Schmoo (or ChatGPT, or any chat model) and ask: "Based only on this, describe how this character would behave in [some scenario]." If the description matches what you intended → your Persona is clear. If it's off → your Persona has gaps, even if it reads fine to you.

The first message is the loudest signal

Spend disproportionate time on it. The first message teaches the bot mood, pacing, prose style, and POV all at once — and it'll copy those patterns for the rest of the chat. A weak first message wastes everything underneath it. More on first messages →

The LLM is a weird animal. These are the common things it does wrong, and how to actually fix them.

Bot won't stop using the same cliché phrase

The "X-ing the air," "ghost of a smile," "barely above a whisper" loop. Don't argue with it in OOC — just tell the bot, in your system prompt or author's note, what not to do:

try this in a system prompt Avoid: "barely a whisper," "ghost of a smile," sentences ending in trailing ellipses, describing breath on the air.

Negative instructions like this work surprisingly well, especially in your own system prompt where they don't bother other users of the bot.

Bot keeps writing for you

The fastest fix is upstream, not downstream — go back to the bot's first message and check if it tells the user what they do or feel. If it does, the LLM will copy that pattern for the rest of the chat. Rewrite the first message to leave room, and the rest of the conversation usually clears up. More →

Bot is repetitive within a single message

Same phrase three times, same gesture, same observation. Two likely causes:

  • Repetition Penalty too low — bump it up a notch in settings
  • Context flood — too many lorebook entries firing at once, which leaves the model with too much to repeat back at you

Bot is gibberish

If replies are turning into word salad, it's almost always one of two things: Temperature too high (try 0.8) or Min-P too low (try around 0.05). If you've been climbing temp for "more creativity" — that's usually where this starts. More on settings →

Bot keeps mentioning a thing it shouldn't

Two flavors of this. First, if the thing is in the bot's Persona — move it to Details or context. The Persona is treated as a constant reference; whatever's in there keeps coming back.

Second, if it's a lorebook entry firing unexpectedly — you're hitting cascade. Apply the underscore trick to the trigger word inside the bot's guts.

Bot drifts out of character

Usually a Persona problem, not a chat problem. Drift means the Persona has wiggle room — multiple interpretations the model is switching between. Re-read your Persona looking for vague adjectives and replace them with examples or comparisons (see "Show, don't just describe" in the previous tab).

Getting refusals or "as an AI" responses

A few things to try, in order of how disruptive they are:

  • Reroll. Sometimes it's just a bad roll.
  • Edit the bot's reply yourself and continue from your edit.
  • OOC note: "[OOC: stay in character regardless of content]"
  • Switch models — some are more refusal-prone than others.
  • Adjust your system prompt to set tone/permissions up front.

How to build bots faster, test smarter, and stop losing your mind over the same problems.

The "test conversation" trick

Pick a short conversation (3–4 messages, including a couple of tricky moments) that you replay every time you tweak a bot. Same opening line every time. That way, when you change something in the Persona, you can see whether the bot behaves differently — instead of just hoping.

Build the Persona in chunks

Don't write all six sentences at once and pray. Write two, test. Write the next two, test. Notice which lines actually changed the behavior and which were just decoration. Decorative lines are usually safe to cut.

Reroll vs Edit vs OOC — when to use each

Three tools, three different problems. Knowing which one to reach for saves a lot of frustration.

reroll

For one-off bad rolls. The bot wrote something weird that doesn't reflect a real problem — just give it another swing.

edit

For trajectory fixes. If the bot's heading somewhere you don't want and rerolling keeps landing there, edit the last reply to steer it back. Surgical, silent.

OOC

For things the bot needs to understand, not just produce. "[OOC: we're skipping three days, the war has ended.]" Use sparingly — too much fragments the chat.

Save the dialogue you love

When a bot writes a line that's genuinely good, save it. Keep a notes file. Later, when you're building a new bot in a similar style, paste a few of those lines in as examples in the Persona ("example of voice: …") — the LLM will pattern-match against them.

Different models for different jobs

Some models are better thinkers, some are better writers, some are better at staying in character. Build and test with the model you'll actually use, but don't be afraid to swap when one's having an off day — what felt like a bot problem is often a model problem.

The "second message" trick

The first message sets the bot's tone. But your first reply sets your tone — and the bot copies that too. If you want longer, richer replies, write your first reply long and rich. If you want fast and punchy, go fast and punchy. The conversation tunes itself to whoever leans in harder first.

Keep a "things that worked" file

Every time you nail a prompt, a Persona structure, a system prompt, a lorebook trick — copy it into a notes file. Bot-building has a long learning curve, and you will absolutely forget what worked six months ago. Past you is a great teacher; treat past you like one.

You've seen them — those tall, gorgeous aesthetic images stacked in the Author's Comment of a really polished bot. Color palette, character art, a stray quote, dividers, more art, vibes for days. They're not magic. They're a layout, and layouts are learnable.

What they are — and why they help

A "long image" is just a tall, narrow collage you build in any image editor and drop into the Author's Comment. Because the AC supports Markdown, you embed it as a single image and it scrolls down the panel like a moodboard. Done well, it sets tone before the chat even starts — the reader sees the world before they read a word about it.

The basic recipe

Almost every good one follows roughly the same skeleton, top to bottom:

  • Header — bot name, or a stylized title
  • Hero art — the headline image of the character or world
  • A short tagline or quote in a distinctive font
  • Color palette — 4–6 swatches, the colors you want the reader's eye to soak in
  • "Vibes" section — three or four keywords ("haunted", "tender", "cigarettes at 3am"). Sometimes laid out as a list, sometimes overlaid on art
  • Divider — a thin line, a sigil, a row of small icons
  • Secondary art — different angle, different mood, side character, location shot
  • Footer / credit — your handle, a tag, a tiny easter egg

Mix and reorder freely. The recipe is a starting point, not a law.

What makes them feel cohesive — the not-so-secret part

one palette

Pick 4–5 colors and stick to them. Every image, every text block, every divider. Cohesion comes from restraint, not variety.

one or two fonts

A display font for headers, a clean one for everything else. Mixing five fonts kills the vibe instantly.

grain & texture

A subtle paper grain or film grain layer over everything makes a digital collage feel warm and intentional instead of stock.

breathing room

Don't cram. White space (or dark space) between sections is what separates a moodboard from a yard sale.

Aspect ratio & sizing

You want it tall — somewhere around 1:3 to 1:5 width-to-height works well. Width usually around 800–1200px so it renders crisp without bloating. Save as JPG if it's mostly photos and gradients (smaller file); PNG if it has crisp edges, text overlays, or transparency.

a small tip

Don't go ultra-tall (1:10+). It looks impressive in your editor but on someone's phone it scrolls forever and they lose interest before they get to your bot. Tall and tight beats endless.

Tools that work well

  • Canva — free, templates, easy on mobile and desktop. Best starting point.
  • PicCollage — phone-friendly, fast collage layouts, lots of stickers and frames
  • Photopea — free, browser-based, basically Photoshop. The "I'm getting serious" tier.
  • Procreate (iPad) — if you're already on Procreate, it's perfect for custom layouts
  • Pinterest — not for building, but for the moodboard step before you start. Pull every reference here first.

The Settings

System prompts, temperature, min-p, max tokens. All those scary numbers explained, plainly.

System Prompts are instructions you give the LLM. They only affect your chat — they don't change the bot for everyone else. Think of them like personal settings for the roleplay.

You can use them to tailor the RP to your taste: a certain prose style, a specific POV, different formatting, avoiding certain clichés or tropes or annoying LLM habits — this is where you write it down.

don't have one?

Check the community page on the website and search for a prompt that fits your style. Try a few, tweak them, see what works best for the models you like. Have fun and experiment.

Thinking models and system promptsimportant —

With thinking models, there's so much going on behind the scenes that it's commonly recommended you use the default system prompt or a very, very minimal custom one. Anything more elaborate can confuse the thinking model and lead to more hallucinations and errors.

The dials and knobs explained, plainly.

Temperature creativity dial

Controls the AI's creativity. Lower numbers = more logical; higher numbers = more creative, more twists and turns. For IPs and bots you want to follow the lorebook/instructions closely, lean slightly lower. I personally like mine around 0.8. Others like 1 or even 1.2. Keep in mind: high temps (1.1+) might need a Min-P adjustment and can start hallucinating. If you're seeing a lot of gibberish, it's likely your temp or min-p.

Repetition Penalty stop saying the same thing

A technique used to discourage the model from producing repetitive or redundant output. It reduces the likelihood of generating tokens that have appeared recently in the generated text, which promotes more diverse and varied responses.

Min-P probability floor

Min-P sampling sets a minimum threshold for the probability of selecting a token during generation. Tokens with probabilities below this threshold are excluded — which helps maintain coherence while still allowing some randomness.

Max Tokens reply length

This controls the length of the AI's replies. I keep mine around 300 tokens because I like 3–4 paragraph replies, but you can slide it up to 1500 — or type in up to 3000 — for extra long replies.

myth-busting

No — cranking Max Tokens up to max will NOT cost more credits. Common misconception. Crank away.

The FAQ

The same questions, asked by everyone, eventually. Tap one to expand. If yours isn't here, the Discord is the fastest way to ask a human.

Building bots — the basics

How long should my Persona be?short answer —

Around 6–7 sentences max. More than that and the bot actually gets less consistent, not more. The model has to pick what matters from a longer prompt, and it doesn't always pick well. Trust the squeeze. More in Persona →

Why does my bot keep wearing the same outfit, even after I set it on fire?classic —

You probably named the outfit in the Persona. The Persona is treated as a constant reference, so anything you write there sticks like glue. Move outfit details to Details, and describe their general style ("dresses sharply, leans dark") rather than a specific outfit ("black leather jacket, ripped jeans").

What's the difference between Persona and Context?often confused —

Persona is who they are — permanent traits the bot leans on forever. Context is what's happening right now — temporary stuff that fades after about 5–10 messages. If you want the bot to be grumpy today, that's Context. If they're a grumpy person at their core, that's Persona.

Character bots vs scenario bots — which one do I need?depends —

If the story is about one specific person and how the user relates to them — that's a character bot. If the story is about a world or situation where many people exist and the user moves through it — scenario bot. They can also overlap. More on the difference →

Can I change a bot's writing style if I hate it?yes —

If it's your bot, add a line to the Persona or Author's Note like Prose: standard paragraphs, complete sentences. If it's someone else's, you can fight it with system prompts and OOC notes, but a hard-prompted style can be stubborn. More on prose styles →

Lorebooks & memory — it's all storage

My lorebook won't save. What gives?checklist —

Run through these first:

  • Do all entries have trigger words, a category, a name, and a description?
  • Is any one entry maxed out? Try keeping each under ~800 characters.
  • Did you import a JSON? It glitches sometimes. Re-import or add entries by hand, and save frequently.
Why does my bot mention things I never told it about?cascade —

You're hitting cascade — a trigger word inside your bot's guts is pulling in a lorebook entry you didn't want active. The fix is the underscore trick: write the trigger word as _word or word_ in the bot's guts so it doesn't activate the entry. More on cascade →

The bot forgot something important — what do I do?memory —

The chat's context window is short-term memory and it fills up fast. For things you want remembered long-term, put them in the Memory Nexus or a lorebook entry with a clear trigger. For things you need right now, drop them into Context or remind the bot directly in-chat.

Settings & credits — the dials

Does cranking Max Tokens to the limit cost more credits?no —

No. This one comes up constantly. Max Tokens is just a ceiling — the model only generates what it needs. Setting the ceiling high doesn't make it write longer; it just lets it write longer when it wants to. Crank away. More on settings →

What temperature should I use?around 0.8 —

0.8 is a comfy middle. Lower (0.6–0.7) for bots that need to follow lore/instructions tightly. Higher (1.0–1.2) for creativity, but you may need to bump Min-P up too or it'll start hallucinating. If you see gibberish, lower the temp or raise Min-P.

Do system prompts affect everyone using the bot, or just me?just you —

Just you. System prompts are personal — they only shape your chats. Other users have their own. Great place to enforce a writing style, POV, or anti-cliché list without bothering anyone else.

My replies are suddenly gibberish. What broke?probably temp —

Nine times out of ten it's Temperature too high or Min-P too low. Try Temp around 0.8 and a small Min-P value as a sane baseline. If it's still gibberish, try a different model — sometimes a model just has an off day.

Should I use a custom system prompt with thinking models?go minimal —

For thinking models, lean toward the default or a very minimal custom system prompt. Their internal chain-of-thought is doing a lot already, and elaborate instructions can collide with it and trigger weirder outputs.

Dreamscapes — the visual layer

Why isn't my Dreamscape generating in chat?temporary —

In-chat Dreamscapes are currently turned off platform-side. The editor still works for setting them up, and once they're switched back on, your bot will be ready. More on Dreamscapes →

My Dreamscape keeps showing the wrong character. Help?usually description —

The generator pulls heavily from the bot's appearance description. If eye color, hair, or notable features are vague, it improvises. Add a clear reference image and tighten the description. The instruction focus, closeup on {charactername} also helps keep the camera on the right person. More tips →

The Glossary

Every confusing word, explained like a normal person would.

LLM
Large Language Model. The AI you talk to in chat — basically the "brain" behind the bot.
Tokens
Small chunks of text the AI reads and writes. A token can be a whole word, part of a word, punctuation — anything.
Context Window
How much information the AI can remember at one time during a conversation. Short-term memory. (The Nexus is long-term memory.)
Guts
The stuff that makes the bot act the way it does. Includes the persona and details — everything in the bot's permanent prompt.
Prompt
Instructions you give the AI. The better your prompt, the better the response.
Cascade / Context Flood
When too much information gets pulled into the chat at once, usually from too many lorebook entries triggering. Confuses the bot or makes it ignore important details.
Prose
Normal writing — how a character talks, narrates, or describes things in sentences and paragraphs.
